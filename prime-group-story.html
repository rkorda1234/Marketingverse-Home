A New Story for Prime Group

Your rooms are already better than the story you are telling about them. And with a few small additions, people will remember them for years.

Section 02 · What we found

Everything you need already exists. Nobody is using it.

Four days on the island. I walked through every venue, ate at Lucky Rabbit, Yallah and Uncle Liu's, and spent time in the rooms your competitors are filling.

We talked through the brand, the audience, what you have already tried, and what has been frustrating you. Alongside that, I went through every website, every Google profile, and the numbers you shared.

I also drank more in four days than I usually manage in a month, which I now understand is simply how business gets done on that island.

Your business is local first

Lucky Rabbit is 80 percent local. San Si Wu and Yallah are 95 percent. Smash is 70 percent. Uncle Liu's and Coconut Room split evenly.

Only Prime Kitchen is a visitor business, at 90 percent.

That changes where the money should go. Six of seven venues live on a resident population of 91,362, which means the job is frequency and loyalty before it is discovery.

It also explains why August only drops 50 percent when visitor arrivals fall much further. The local base is carrying you through the low season, and nothing in the marketing speaks to them directly.

This is not an argument against tourists. It is an argument about the order things happen in.

The visitors worth having are the ones with money and a few nights to fill, and they do not want the place built for tourists. They want the place locals actually go. So the way to reach them is to make the rooms conspicuously full of locals, then make sure the people they ask, their villa host, their concierge, their driver, know your name.

Fill the room with locals and the right visitors follow. Chase visitors first and you usually get neither.

Two constraints that shape everything

Your visitors have money and are cautious about food. They are not going to gamble a whole dinner on a cuisine they do not know.

So every table needs two things on it. The safe order, which is what gets them through the door and what they will happily pay for. And the story order, which is small, shareable, and the thing they talk about afterward.

The trick is making the second one low risk. A small plate to share, or a bite that arrives without being asked for, means nobody is betting their meal on it. They still get to say they tried something, and that is the part they repeat when someone asks where to eat.

And the liquor law runs to 2am Monday through Friday, but only to midnight on Saturday and Sunday. That makes Thursday the only night of the week you can run late, and almost nothing on the island is built around it.

Section 05 · The proposal

Stop paying for attention. Let the experience earn it instead.

Everything above is repair. This is the part that changes the business.

The food keeps being marketed, because it is genuinely very good and it is the reason people come back. But it cannot carry the whole story on its own. What we add alongside it are experiences people want to tell each other about, and those are what the marketing concentrates on.

Section 05A · The group

Four things turn seven businesses into one.

Right now these read as seven unrelated businesses.

  • Locals First. In high season, your regulars get squeezed out by visitors. We give them priority instead: booking windows, held tables, walk in priority, in December when everyone else is turning them away. It costs nothing, we did not come across anyone else doing it, and it is the kind of thing people repeat.
  • The Prime Group Night. One night a quarter, the whole group's menu available in one room. Szechuan, Filipino, sushi, ramen, burgers, noodles, all at one table. Most tables of six never agree on one cuisine. For one night a quarter, they do not have to. It is also the only idea that lets a customer taste the entire group in one sitting.
  • The Beach Cookout. Free, no ticket, and no permit required so long as nobody is charged. DJ, BBQ, the team cooking. People eat, meet the team, and attach a feeling to the Prime name rather than to one venue. Everything else in marketing asks for something. This gives first.
  • Knowing people properly. On an island where everyone knows everyone, being greeted by name is not special. What is: being anticipated, being remembered when you are not there, and having your status extend to whoever you bring. We build the system that makes that possible across all seven venues.
  • The kids box. Not one venue, all of them. A properly designed box with good food and something to do, the same across the group, recognisable anywhere. Kids decide where families eat, and right now nobody on the island owns that. It is cheap, it travels between venues, and it becomes something people associate with the Prime name rather than with one room.
  • The starter that costs nothing. A small bite handed over while people wait or as they sit, made from trim and offcuts the kitchen already has. Pork belly ends, fish collars, vegetable trim, day of bread. It does three things at once: every meal starts with a gift, waste goes down, and it is the risk free way to put something unfamiliar in front of a cautious guest, because nobody is betting their dinner on a bite they did not order.
  • The sticker. Take a photo of the table, turn it into a WhatsApp sticker, send it to them. You get their number, they get something genuinely fun, and it ends up in group chats where your venue is the punchline rather than an advert. On an island where WhatsApp is how everything happens, that is a better list than any email signup form.
  • A standing content pillar: the people behind the plate. Farmers, fishermen, importers, suppliers, and your own team. It explains why food costs what it does here, it rewards the team publicly, and on an island this size those people bring their own audience.
Section 05B · The venues

Every venue gets three moments: arrival, middle, departure.

Something when they arrive, something in the middle, something on the way out. Small, consistent, and cheap. A thing that happens every time becomes a signature. A thing that happens once is a stunt.

Uncle Liu's

Local professionals, 32 to 50. Willing to pay, not driven by trend, and they do not want a quiet room.

The play

The room gets louder as the night goes on, deliberately rather than by accident. Then The Turn, a Thursday where dinner becomes a party at 9pm. Drums mark the moment, the curtain opens, the room shifts. Thursday because it is the only night the law lets you run to 2am. The small room works in your favour here, because a full room reads as a party long before a larger one would.

The story

Choose your chopsticks from a tray on arrival. A dish finished at the table, broth poured or sauce lit in front of them. A handwritten proverb card with the check, in someone's actual handwriting.

Coconut Room

The Filipino community, families, celebrations. Affordable is not negotiable.

The play

Own the community's calendar. Every birthday, every anniversary, every milestone. Tourists come because the room is full of the people whose food it is, so protect that rather than diluting it.

The story

A welcome shot for the table. The celebration moment, done loudly, with a song and a plate. Choc Nut or White Rabbit candy with the check, which costs pennies and means something specific to half the room.

Lucky Rabbit

21 to 40, neighbourhood crowd, there for the atmosphere and the escape.

The play

Karaoke is the only thing keeping people drinking past eleven, so build on it rather than treating it as one night. Take the best performances to a Cayman radio station weekly, which gives people a reason to sing and puts the venue in people's ears without buying ads. Then add nights that suit the room: Japanese bingo, a sexy bingo night. Formats that are loud, participatory and a little silly, which is exactly what the venue already is.

The story

Pick your karaoke song on a slip as you sit. A traditional sake pour at the table, overflowing into the box. Your bottle kept behind the bar with your name on it for next time.

Carnivore Smash

Local, Jamaican, Caymanian. Value, speed, convenience.

The play

The cultural burger works and should continue, but it is not enough on its own. Four additions, all proven elsewhere and all cheap.

  • A secret menu. In-N-Out built a mythology on this and it costs nothing to run. Off-menu items only regulars know about, spread by word of mouth rather than advertising.
  • A ritual with the sauce. Secret Burger Kitchen serves sauce in a syringe instead of a dip cup. Guests inject it themselves, nobody double dips, and they take it home. Cheap, tactile, and endlessly filmed.
  • Own a word. The crisp lacy edge of a smashed patty has a name in the burger world, meat lace. Name yours and use it every time.
  • Limited drops. In-N-Out ran a four hour pop-up in Sydney, announced the day before, 300 burgers, sold out before it opened. Park the truck somewhere unexpected, announce it the night before, sell out, leave.
The story

They call your name rather than a number. The flattop positioned so people watch the smash. A bite thrown in that nobody asked for.

San Si Wu

Nostalgic. People who know it and have a favourite order.

The play

This is where Lucas made his name. A hole in the wall, raw and a bit rough, and that was the charm. Delivery stripped all of it out and left only the food. Put the attitude back in the box: packaging that looks like the old place rather than a clean delivery brand, a voice on the label that sounds like the kitchen, and a printed invitation in the bag to something that only exists for people who order, a one night pop-up, a secret address, a night at the plaza kitchen after close. The charm was never the room. It was the feeling of being let in on something. That can be delivered.

The story

A confirmation written by a person, not a bot. Packaging built to be opened, with a note inside. A card with their usual order printed on it, so they know you remembered.

Yallah

Office workers, lunch, Camana Bay. 95 percent local.

The play

It needs a persona before it needs a campaign, and the numbers we have are dine in only. What it can start now is the thing that suits a daily audience: comment on today's post to claim something, or post a photo from the room and show it at the counter. It is the same people every day, so give them a small reason to engage every day.

The story

The shake is the one worth building around: the bowl gets a lid at the end of the line and the customer shakes it themselves. It takes three seconds, it actually mixes the bowl better than a spoon does, and it turns the last step from watching into doing. Two alternatives if the shake does not suit the food: a finishing flourish at the end of the line, a torch on the halloumi or a drizzle poured from height, which sends the smell back down the queue and sells to everyone waiting. Or a bowl of the month designed by a staff member, with their name and face on the board, which ties the menu to the people making it. Then a baklava bite or a mint sweet with the order.

Prime Kitchen

Visitors in villas and on boats. Ninety percent tourist, and the pricing currently moves between $800 and $3,000 for the same service.

The play

Build the referral network. Villa companies already pay off, so extend the same commission model to Airbnb hosts, yacht charters and travel agents. Then split the offer in two, because right now it is only one thing: done for you, the chef arrives, cooks, serves, cleans, disappears, what exists today; and cook with the chef, the chef arrives and the guests cook alongside him. It is dinner and entertainment in the same booking, it fills an evening rather than an hour, and it is worth more per head. A version for kids works even better, because parents on holiday will pay for something that occupies the children and produces a memory. Price both properly and publish the prices. A villa host cannot recommend something when she does not know what it costs.

Section 05C · Corporate catering

The arm with the most room to grow.

This is the one where marketing can help a salesman rather than replace him.

What you are actually selling

An HR rep ordering lunch for forty people is not buying food. She is buying not having to think about it, and she is buying looking good to her team on Friday afternoon.

That reframes the whole pitch. Competing on price against a cheaper caterer is a losing game and you will not win it. Competing on effortlessness and on how the order makes her look is winnable, and nobody in Cayman is doing it.

The reorder is the product

Corporate catering is a habit business. Whoever is easiest to reorder from wins, and most catering ordering is genuinely painful. Phone calls, email chains, a menu PDF, a quote, a confirmation. So we build the opposite.

  • A saved order. She orders once, and every order after that is two taps. Same food, same headcount, same delivery window.
  • A standing order. Friday lunch every week, adjustable until Wednesday. It stops being a decision and becomes a default.
  • One link, all seven venues. Chinese on Monday, burgers on Friday, Mediterranean when someone is watching their diet. Nobody else on the island can offer that from a single order.
  • The office votes. A QR code on the break room wall, or a link in an email to the team, and everyone picks what they want next week. It takes the decision off the rep entirely, it makes the whole office feel consulted, and it tells you exactly what that building likes ordering.
  • AI handles the annoying parts. Dietary requirements remembered, headcount adjusted, and an invoice that has everything accounts payable needs. That email still goes out because finance requires it, but nobody has to read it, because the confirmation people actually see arrives on WhatsApp.

A second reason to order

Lunch is one occasion a week. There is an easier one nobody is selling: the afternoon lift. Fresh juice, fruit, something sweet, delivered mid-afternoon on a Tuesday or a Wednesday. Low cost per head, small enough that it does not need approval from anyone senior, and it lands at the exact hour an office is flagging.

It puts you in the building twice a week instead of once, it is an easy yes for a rep who wants a cheap win with her team, and it fits the argument you are already making, which is that better food makes for a better afternoon.

Getting into the room

They have been hard to reach because you have been reaching for them cold. Three better routes.

  • Feed them first. A dinner for HR managers and office managers. No pitch, just a good night out and a group of people who now know the food. Two in the first year is enough to start the conversation moving.
  • Referral from inside. Your existing corporate clients know their counterparts at other firms. Cayman's corporate world is small and everyone knows everyone, which works in your favour here.
  • Show up where they already are. Chamber of Commerce, HR association events, the fund administrator and law firm circuit.

One caution on incentives

Perks for the person placing the order need care. Cayman's corporate sector is dominated by law firms, fund administrators and insurers, all of which run serious compliance cultures. A personal benefit to a decision maker can read as a kickback, and that can cost you an account rather than win one.

The safer and stronger version is a reward that benefits the whole office. The best one you already have the capability to deliver: order regularly, and your team event is on us. A drinks reception, a team dinner, a holiday party at any of the seven venues. You already offer full buyouts, so the machinery exists.

For the rep it is the easiest internal win available, because she gets to be the person who got the team a party. For you it costs a slow Monday and food at cost rather than cash, and it puts forty people who have only eaten your delivery food inside one of your rooms. A good number of them come back on their own. Set a clear threshold so it feels earned rather than negotiated, and schedule the events on nights that need filling.

The sales tool

Your salesman needs something to open with. We build it: a short presentation covering the food, the presentation standard, the seven venue range, delivery reliability, and the pricing. A sample menu set by headcount and budget. A one page leave behind. And a tasting box that can be dropped at an office so the decision is made with the food in the room rather than on a screen.

We are the only caterer on the island who can feed your office something different every day of the week.

Section 05D · Creators

Stop paying for posts. Build things worth posting about.

A creator invited to a normal dinner posts a plate, because a plate is all that happened. A creator invited to The Turn, or a beach cookout, posts because they had a night. One of those is an advert nobody asked for. The other is a recommendation.

So the experiences are the creator strategy. They just need people in the room when they happen.

Micro only

In a market of 91,362 people, twenty thousand followers who live here or visit here is worth more than two million who never will. Reach is not the metric. Relevance is.

On island, there is a small and genuinely good bench: accounts like Cayman Islands Foodie, Good Eats Cayman, Island Epicurean and Cayman Eats. Island Epicurean is the one to start with. She is Caymanian, she appears on camera, and she has judged competitions and interviewed chefs at Cookout. That is credibility rather than reach, and credibility is what converts a local booking.

Off island, the target is creators in the places your visitors come from, brought in around a specific moment rather than a general invitation. Cookout week in January. A launch night. Something that is actually happening, so there is a reason to fly and a reason to post.

One constraint to respect

Paid creator work in Cayman runs into work permit requirements. The structures that avoid the problem are comped visits with genuine organic posting, contracting through a creator's own company, and paying for content licensing rather than for services performed on the island. Worth a quick legal check before any programme starts.

Two additions to the team.

Strategist

Someone who owns the vision, connects the seven venues into one story, and makes sure every decision serves it. That is me.

On the ground

Section 06 · How it rolls out

Four phases, roughly nine months.

  • Phase 1. Fix and hire. Months 1 and 2. Quick wins shipped: profiles corrected, websites fixed, social redirected, rooms improved. At the same time, we recruit the production coordinator.
  • Phase 2. Build. Months 3 and 4. Design the experiences before announcing them. Produce the content library. Prepare the launches. Nothing goes public until it is ready, because a weak first night is harder to recover from than a late one.
  • Phase 3. Launch and learn. Months 5 to 8. Roll out the experiences one at a time and market them properly. Every one is tested cheaply before it is built permanently.
  • Phase 4. Double down. Month 9 onward. Analyse what worked. Scale it. Adjust what nearly worked. Cut what did not. By this point the decisions are made on data rather than instinct, because for the first time you will have the data.

Section 08 · Closing

The hardest part is behind you.

You told me you could not put the problem into words.

Here is what I think it is. The food is excellent and the rooms are good. What is thin is everything around them. The moments people remember afterward, and the story that gets told when someone asks where to eat.

Both need building. Not one or the other.

The food is done. The ratings are done. The hardest part is behind you.

That does not mean it stops improving. A few small additions to the room and to the meal, the kind people remember years later, and the experience catches up to how good the cooking already is.

What is left is making people feel it before they arrive, and remember it after they leave.
